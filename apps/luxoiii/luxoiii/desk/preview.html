01 / What we built

We made a lamp that responds.

We wanted desk lighting to feel attentive without spoken commands. LuxoIII notices people, welcomes an open palm, and follows a finger across a virtual desk. We run the AI locally so the interaction does not depend on a cloud service.

We see and interpret.

We process the USB camera on the Arduino UNO Q: face detection, palm detection, 21 hand landmarks, and our gesture classifier. We use short gesture holds to avoid reacting to every uncertain frame.

We connect the real and digital.

We host this app on the UNO Q. Your browser renders our full mechanical model, blends its expressions, and shows the camera beside it. Pointing moves the simulated desk light; an open palm brings its attention back to you.

We control what works.

We track the largest face with the physical swivel, limited to ±45°. We switch D7 fully on or off, without PWM, using fresh face and beam-angle checks. Our stepper joints are damaged, so full-arm movement is simulated in this demo.

02 / AI optimization & measured performance

Recorded on our Arduino UNO Q · 13 October 2026

We made every millisecond count.

We cut face + hand acquisition time by 34.9%. We then improved gesture recognition with a small classifier and kept measured processing time essentially unchanged.

Recorded model-processing time; lower is faster. These tests include preprocessing, inference and decoding model outputs. Camera capture, image decoding, network and hardware delays are excluded. Our latest larger-hand priority update is not included; current live processing time is shown above.

What we optimized

  • We tested 1, 2 and 4 CPU threads and chose four per model, running face and hand models sequentially. We kept OpenCV at one thread to avoid competing worker pools.
  • We select face candidates directly from 8-bit outputs and use a cached score lookup. We decode only the candidates we need, preserving the detector’s results.
  • We reuse tracked hand crops. We rejected a more aggressive scan-skipping experiment because it missed additional hands.
  • We replaced gesture rules with a 21 KB network trained on hand landmarks. We kept the image models and their input resolution unchanged.

More accurate gestures, a small added cost

We trained a 21,168-byte classifier on hand landmarks. It adds about 1.2 ms per detected hand and does not run another image model. Our paired test kept total model-workload latency effectively unchanged.

We froze the model before evaluating 210 fresh HaGRID test images from 189 subjects, with no training or validation subjects in the test sample. Pointing improved from 18 to 30 correct hands out of 60.

Hand localization remains the main limit: 82 of 259 annotated hands did not match a prediction. The still-image test does not measure motion blur, live gesture stability or desk-target accuracy.

How we measured the tradeoff

We used the UNO Q CPU with four LiteRT threads per image model, sequential face/hand inference and one OpenCV thread. Our first optimization kept the supplied 8-bit image models, input sizes and detection thresholds unchanged. We paused competing live inference during controlled runs and used six warmup calls for the original detector comparison.

The gesture speed test used the first five images in each of seven categories from a frozen test manifest. We reset hand tracks between old/new/new/old passes. The classifier-only figures average 765 calls per recognizer: 0.023 ms for the original rules and 1.242 ms for the new model. The 183.18 ms result uses different images from the earlier 205.7 ms result, so we do not treat that difference as a speedup.

Our accuracy metric first requires a predicted hand box to overlap its human annotation at IoU ≥ 0.5, then requires the correct gesture. We retained missed hands and negative/no-command hands in the denominator. We trained on 28,819 landmark hands and selected the model with a separate validation split.

The detector optimization increased CPU work per face + hand acquisition slightly, from 583.1 to 602.6 CPU-ms. We did not measure power savings. Its active image models stayed at 6.48 MB, and evaluation-process peak memory stayed about 130 MiB. The new landmark classifier adds about 21 kB of weights.

How the real lamp responds

The real swivel follows the subject within ±45°, with a quiet zone at the center. The digital arm has its own personality. Gestures stay on; Stop pauses the demo.

The physical lamp switches fully on, without PWM, after one second with no detected faces and only when the entire estimated beam is below horizontal. A face, upward tilt, stale data, or Stop switches it off. Beam direction uses the measured joints and shade geometry, assuming a level base. Swivel and beam width are estimates. A missing pitch sensor keeps the light off while tracking can continue.

Keep both hands fully visible and apart. We prioritize the two largest detected palms. If both point, a clearly larger palm takes over; similar sizes keep the current selection stable. If the estimated finger ray misses the desk, fingertip position guides a virtual cursor instead.

Camera calibration & assumptions

The camera moves with the head pivot. Starting assumptions: 100° horizontal view, 16 cm face width, camera 5 cm along the shade axis, base yaw fixed at zero. Adjust these to match the actual camera. A turned face or palm can make distance too large. Board gesture targeting currently uses the default 100° / 8 cm / 5 cm mount calibration; these controls adjust visualization only.

About the model

Model and choreography: full-model-animations · ef1b0d2. Full-body poses from lamp_demo; additive expressions from ELEGNT. Browser animation blends these kinematically; it is not a physics simulation. Cam rotation and plate orbit use the source model’s gear couplings. The model can follow measured pitch joints or animate independently. Face detection runs on the UNO Q; the real swivel tracks the subject after activation. The physical lamp requires no detected faces and a downward beam. No stepper movement commands are sent. Finger-ray targets are approximate. The virtual cursor maps fingertip image position onto the simulated desk; it does not locate a real object.
